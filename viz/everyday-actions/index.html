Frequency and experienced affect

The default view uses one study for both axes: 909 employed women in the Day Reconstruction Method study (Kahneman et al., 2004). They reported each episode of a working day and rated how they felt. Other views bring in national time-use estimates from the American Time Use Survey. Those combine two populations and are marked as such.

      A regret frontier

      A hundred common actions, from texting a friend to buying a home, sorted by two properties that change how a mistake feels afterwards. One is how easily the action can be undone. The other is whether the chance to act closes soon. Regret studies find that people regret actions more in the short run and inactions more in the long run (Gilovich & Medvec, 1995; replicated with a different shape by Richardson & Gilovich, 2023). That suggests running cheap experiments on actions that are both reversible and time-limited.

      These positions are authored codes on 1–5 scales, not measurements. No dataset rates these 100 actions on reversibility or time pressure. The coding rubric is in the methodology below. Blue points have an experiment behind them, usually showing that people misjudge how the action will go; click one to read it.

        Downside, upside and what you learn

        The same hundred actions placed by the plausible worst outcome (horizontal) against the plausible best outcome (vertical). Larger circles mark actions that reveal more about your preferences or about other people when done once. Low-downside, high-upside actions sit top-left, and large circles there are the cheapest ways to learn something.

          Considered, never done

          Some things come back to mind again and again without getting done. Each return costs a little attention. Laboratory work finds that unfinished goals keep producing intrusive thoughts, and that writing a specific plan stops the intrusion even before the goal is met (Masicampo & Baumeister, 2011). This ledger adds up the time spent reconsidering and places each item by what doing it would take.

          Sources and methodology

          Where each axis comes from

          • Share reporting it on a workday, positive, negative, tired, impatient, competent, and mean hours: Kahneman, Krueger, Schkade, Schwarz & Stone (2004), Science 306:1776, Table 1, transcribed cell by cell. Ratings run from 0 (not at all) to 6 (very much). Positive affect averages happy, warm/friendly and enjoying myself. Negative affect averages six negative descriptors. Net affect is the paper's own difference, positive minus negative, and is labelled as a transformation.
          • Share doing it on an average day and minutes when done: American Time Use Survey microdata, 2014–2016, n = 32,990 respondents age 15+. Computed with the final weight from the BLS files redistributed in the CRAN atus package. The code is derive_atus.py. Run on 2015 alone, the same code reproduces the BLS 2015 release: TV 2.8 h/day, socializing 41 min/day.

          How activities were cross-walked

          Every activity keeps its source label next to its normalized name (crosswalk.csv). A match is close when the definitions agree apart from primary versus secondary activity. It is partial when the constructs differ, and partial matches are drawn hollow. Intimate relations and napping have no ATUS counterpart and appear only in DRM-only views. Sleeping, reading, volunteering, gaming, studying, lawn care and adult care have no DRM counterpart and appear only in ATUS-only views. Nothing was merged to gain points.

          To test the crosswalk, ATUS was restricted to employed women 18+ who worked on a weekday diary day, which matches the DRM sample definition. Where the definitions match, the two surveys agree: commuting 0.87 vs 0.86, eating 0.94 vs 0.94, preparing food 0.62 vs 0.64, exercising 0.16 vs 0.18. They diverge badly for socializing (0.65 vs 0.32), relaxing (0.77 vs 0.19) and phone calls (0.61 vs 0.13). The DRM counted every activity ticked for an episode, while ATUS records only the main one.

          Differences in populations and scales

          The DRM sample is a 2004 convenience sample of employed women describing a working day. ATUS is nationally representative of people 15+ across all days of the week, 2014–2016. A view that pairs ATUS frequency with DRM affect places one population's feelings against another population's habits. The page says so whenever such a view is selected. Affect ratings are shown on their original 0–6 scales and are not rescaled to 0–100. The median lines are medians of the points currently shown.

          What is not here, and why

          Meaning, stress and pain by activity exist in the ATUS Well-Being Module (2010, 2012, 2013, 2021; 0–6 ratings of happy, meaningful, sad, stressed, tired and pain) and in the Princeton Affect and Time Survey. Neither could be retrieved from primary files in this build. Their values were therefore left out rather than retyped from secondary summaries, so there is no meaning or stress axis yet. The Well-Being Module is the next source to add.

          The decision charts are interpretation

          Reversibility, time sensitivity, downside, upside and information gained are ordinal codes written for this page (decisions.csv). Rubric, 1 → 5:

          • Reversibility: permanent → undo costs years → undo costs money or friction → small residue (a sent message) → fully repeatable.
          • Time sensitivity: the same value any time this year → value fades over months → over weeks → over days → the window closes within hours or days.
          • Downside and upside: a few minutes or brief awkwardness → recoverable loss or gain over weeks → months → years → lasting, life-course.
          • Information: learn nothing new → something about the task → about your own preferences → about other people's responses → resolves a major uncertainty.

          The frequency groupings (daily, weekly and so on) are authored too. Where an action belongs to an ATUS category, the detail panel shows that category's national participation rate as context, not as the rate for the action itself. Gilbert & Ebert (2002) is a counterweight: people prefer reversible choices but end up less satisfied with them. Reversibility makes an experiment cheap, not free.
