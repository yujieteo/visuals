How English Grammar Works

An interactive guide to the analysis in The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language

Grammatical category and syntactic function are different: inspect what an expression is, what role it has, and how it fits into a larger structure.

Start here: Kim laughed.

Two separate questions get two separate answers. Kim is a noun (its category at word level), and that noun is the head of a one-word noun phrase. The NP Kim, not the noun on its own, is the subject of the clause. Likewise laughed is a verb functioning as predicator, and the one-word VP it heads is the predicate.

Chapters and concepts

Chapter titles follow the book.

  1. Chapter 1. Preliminaries pp. 1–42
    • Category and function Ch. 1 §4.2.2 Syntactic categories
      Every expression can be described twice: by its category (what kind of expression it is, such as noun or NP) and by its function (the role it plays in a larger expression, such as subject or head). The two vary independently.
    • Constituent structure Ch. 1 §4.2.1 Constituent structure
      Sentences are not flat strings of words. Words group into constituents, which group into larger constituents, and the same words can sometimes be grouped in two ways.
    • Heads and dependents Ch. 1 §4.2.3 Grammatical constructions and functions
      Most constituents have a head, the element that determines what kind of phrase it is, and dependents that attach to it: complements, modifiers and (in the NP) a determiner.
    • Words and lexemes Ch. 1 §4.3 Morphology, inflectional and lexical
      Takes, took and taken are three different words but forms of one lexeme, take. Inflection produces word forms of a lexeme; lexical word-formation creates new lexemes.
  2. Chapter 2. Syntactic overview pp. 43–70
    • Sentence and clause Ch. 2 §1 Sentence and clause
      A sentence is the largest unit of syntax; a clause is a construction centrally consisting of a subject and a predicate. A sentence may be one clause, contain clauses inside clauses, or be a coordination of clauses.
    • Canonical and non-canonical clauses Ch. 2 §2 Canonical and non-canonical clauses
      Canonical clauses are the most elementary kind: main, declarative, positive, active and in basic order. Others, such as subordinate, interrogative, passive or extraposed clauses, are described by how they differ from canonical ones.
  3. Chapter 3. The verb pp. 71–212
    • Verb inflection Ch. 3 §1.1 Summary presentation of the categories
      Verbs have six inflectional forms: preterite (took), 3rd singular present (takes), plain present (take), plain form (take), gerund-participle (taking) and past participle (taken).
    • The gerund-participle Ch. 3 §1.4 The gerund-participle
      Verbs in -ing are one inflectional form, the gerund-participle, whether they follow progressive be or head a subject clause. Separately, some -ing words are nouns.
    • Finiteness Ch. 3 §1.8 Primary and secondary forms and the category of finiteness
      Finiteness is treated as a property of clauses. Clauses with a tensed verb are finite; so are imperative and subjunctive clauses, although their verb is the untensed plain form.
    • Auxiliary verbs Ch. 3 §2 Auxiliary verbs
      Auxiliaries such as be, have, do and the modals are verbs with special syntax (inversion, negation with not, emphatic polarity). In the analysis followed here they head the VP and take a non-finite clause as complement.
  4. Chapter 4. The clause: complements pp. 213–322
    • Subject, predicate and predicator Ch. 4 §1 Elements of clause structure: an overview
      A canonical clause consists of a subject and a predicate. The predicate is a VP whose head, the verb, is the predicator; complements and adjuncts are its dependents.
    • Complements vs adjuncts Ch. 4 §1.2 Complements vs adjuncts
      Complements are licensed by a particular head (put needs a location; rely needs on). Adjuncts are not selected and can be added more freely. The same phrase may be either, depending on the head.
    • The subject Ch. 4 §3 The subject
      The subject is identified syntactically: in canonical clauses it precedes the verb, the verb agrees with it, and it inverts with an auxiliary in questions. It is often, but not always, the agent.
    • Semantic roles and subject Ch. 4 §2 Semantic roles
      Agent, patient, instrument and recipient are semantic roles. Subject and object are syntactic functions. The two are related, but a subject need not be an agent, and an agent need not be a subject.
    • Objects Ch. 4 §4 Direct and indirect objects
      An object is an NP complement in the VP, typically after the verb, that can usually become the subject of a passive. Content clauses after verbs like know are complements, not objects.
    • Ditransitive clauses and indirect objects Ch. 4 §4.3 Ditransitive clauses
      Ditransitive clauses have two objects: the indirect object (typically recipient) and then the direct object. A to-PP expressing the recipient is not an indirect object.
    • Predicative complements Ch. 4 §5 Predicatives and related elements
      A predicative complement describes or identifies the subject (Kim became a teacher) or the object (made Pat angry). It may be an AdjP, NP or PP, and it is not an object.
    • Ascriptive and specifying be Ch. 4 §5.5.1 Ascriptive and specifying uses of be
      Be has two main uses with a predicative complement: ascribing a property (Kim is a doctor) and specifying a value (The winner was Kim).
    • Prepositional verbs Ch. 4 §6.1 Prepositional verbs
      Some verbs select a PP headed by a particular preposition (rely on, look at). The NP is the complement of the preposition, not an object of the verb.
    • The verb–particle–object construction Ch. 4 §6.2 The ‘verb – particle – object’ construction
      In looked up the word, up is a particle: a one-word PP complement that can come before or after the object. It is not the head of a PP containing the object.
    • The passive Ch. 4 §3.2.1 The passive and preposing constructions
      In a passive clause the subject corresponds to the object of the active, the verb is be (or get) plus a past participle, and the agent is optional, in a by-phrase.
  5. Chapter 5. Nouns and noun phrases pp. 323–524
    • Nouns and pronouns Ch. 5 §1 Distinctive properties of nouns and NPs
      Nouns head noun phrases. Proper nouns and pronouns are subclasses of noun: she and Kim head NPs just as dog does.
    • Noun phrase structure Ch. 5 §2 Overview of noun classes and NP structure
      An NP may contain a determiner and a head nominal; the nominal contains the head noun with its modifiers and complements.
    • Determiner and determinative Ch. 5 §4 The determiner function
      Determiner is a function in the NP; determinative is a category of words (the, a, some, every, enough, three). Determinatives are often determiners, but each can occur in the other's absence.
    • Determinatives Ch. 5 §7 Other determinatives
      Determinatives are a closed category including the articles, demonstratives, numerals and quantifying words. Several can also modify adjectives and adverbs.
    • Genitives as determiners Ch. 5 §4 The determiner function
      A genitive NP such as Kim's or my can fill the determiner function. It makes the NP definite, like the, and is called a subject-determiner.
    • Fused heads Ch. 5 Nouns and noun phrases
      Sometimes one word does two jobs in the NP: most in most stayed is both determiner and head (fused determiner-head); blue in the blue is both modifier and head (fused modifier-head).
  6. Chapter 6. Adjectives and adverbs pp. 525–596
    • Adjectives Ch. 6 §2 Criterial properties of adjectives
      Adjectives are identified by function and form: they modify nouns attributively, serve as predicative complements, are often gradable and take adverbs like very.
    • Attributive, predicative and postpositive Ch. 6 §4 Restricted function for adjectives
      Most adjectives work both before a noun (attributive) and after be (predicative). Some are attributive-only (main), some never attributive (afraid), and a few follow the noun (members present).
    • Adjective phrases Ch. 6 §3 The structure of AdjPs
      An AdjP has an adjective head with optional modifiers (very, much) and complements (of chocolate, to help).
    • Adverbs and adverb phrases Ch. 6 §5 Adverbs: delimitation of the category
      Adverbs modify verbs, adjectives and other adverbs, but not nouns. The category is narrower here than in traditional grammar: many traditional 'adverbs' are prepositions.
  7. Chapter 7. Prepositions and preposition phrases pp. 597–662
    • Prepositions Ch. 7 §1 The category of prepositions
      Prepositions head PPs and, in the analysis followed here, may take an NP, a clause or no complement at all. Before, after and because are prepositions throughout.
    • Preposition phrases Ch. 7 §5 The structure and functions of PPs
      A PP has a preposition head and usually a complement. PPs serve as complements, adjuncts, predicative complements and modifiers in NPs.
    • Intransitive prepositions Ch. 7 §2.4 Prepositions vs adverbs
      Words such as outside, before and up, used without a complement, are prepositions that happen to be intransitive, not adverbs.
  8. Chapter 8. The clause: adjuncts pp. 663–784
    • Adjuncts Ch. 8 §1 Preliminaries
      Adjuncts add information about manner, means, instrument, time, place, frequency, reason and more. They may be AdvPs, PPs, NPs or clauses, and can occur at the front, middle or end.
  9. Chapter 9. Negation pp. 785–850
    • Clause polarity Ch. 9 §1.1 Tests for clause polarity
      A clause as a whole is positive or negative, and simple tests tell which: a negative clause takes a positive tag (… is she?) and can be continued with neither. Negative meaning inside a word does not by itself make the clause negative.
    • Verbal negation Ch. 9 §2 Verbal negation
      In verbal negation the marker of negation goes with the verb: a negative auxiliary form such as didn't or isn't, or not after an auxiliary. A lexical verb needs the auxiliary do to be negated.
    • Non-verbal negation Ch. 9 §3 Non-verbal negation
      Negation can also be marked away from the verb: by not modifying a phrase, or by an absolute negator such as no, nobody, nothing or never. It may make the whole clause negative (clausal) or stay inside its phrase (subclausal).
    • Approximate negators Ch. 9 §3.3 The approximate negators
      Few, little, rarely, seldom, hardly, scarcely and barely express a small amount rather than none, yet they pattern with negators: a clause containing one usually counts as negative by the tests.
    • Scope of negation Ch. 9 §1.3 Scope and focus of negation
      Negation has scope over part of a clause's meaning. An adjunct can fall inside the scope of the negation or outside it, and its position often shows which.
    • Polarity-sensitive items Ch. 9 §4 Polarity-sensitive items
      Some items are sensitive to polarity. Negatively-oriented ones (NPIs) such as any, ever and yet need a negative or other non-affirmative context; positively-oriented ones (PPIs) such as some and already resist negation.
  10. Chapter 10. Clause type and illocutionary force pp. 851–946
    • Clause type Ch. 10 §2 Distinctive grammatical properties of the major clause types
      Clause type is a grammatical system: declarative, closed interrogative, open interrogative, exclamative and imperative clauses differ in form.
    • Clause type and illocutionary force Ch. 10 §3.1 Illocutionary force
      Clause type is form; illocutionary force (statement, question, directive) is what a speaker does with it. A declarative can be a request, and an imperative can be a wish.
  11. Chapter 11. Content clauses and reported speech pp. 947–1030
    • Subordinators Ch. 11 §3.2 The syntactic category of subordinator
      Subordinators (that, whether, interrogative if, for, and infinitival to) mark a clause as subordinate. They are markers, not heads, and they are a much smaller class than traditional 'subordinating conjunctions'.
    • Declarative content clauses Ch. 11 §4 Functions of declarative content clauses
      Content clauses are the default kind of subordinate finite clause. Declarative ones, often introduced by that, serve as complements of verbs, nouns, adjectives and prepositions, and as subjects.
    • Interrogative content clauses Ch. 11 §5 Interrogative content clauses
      Subordinate interrogatives express questions: closed ones are marked by whether or if; open ones begin with an interrogative phrase in prenucleus position.
  12. Chapter 12. Relative constructions and unbounded dependencies pp. 1031–1096
    • Relative clauses Ch. 12 §2.1 Formal types: wh, that, and bare relatives
      A relative clause contains a gap linked to an antecedent. Wh relatives have a relative word (who, which); that relatives use the subordinator that; bare relatives have neither.
    • Integrated and supplementary relatives Ch. 12 §4 The distinction between integrated and supplementary relative clauses
      Integrated relatives are part of the NP and usually help identify the referent. Supplementary relatives add separate information, are set off prosodically and by punctuation, and are supplements.
    • Fused relatives Ch. 12 §6 The fused relative construction
      In Kim ate what Pat cooked, what Pat cooked is an NP in which what is both head of the NP and the relative phrase of the clause: a fused relative.
  13. Chapter 13. Comparative constructions pp. 1097–1170
    • Comparison of equality and inequality Ch. 13 §1.1 Two cross-cutting distinctions: scalar vs non-scalar, equality vs inequality
      A comparison states either a difference (inequality: taller than, less often than) or a sameness (equality: as tall as). This cuts across a second distinction, between scalar comparison of degrees and non-scalar comparison of identity or likeness.
    • Comparative complements and governors Ch. 13 §1.3 Comparative complements, comparative governors, and comparative phrases
      A comparative complement such as than Pat or as Pat is licensed by a comparative governor: more, less, as, same, or a comparative inflection. Than and as are treated as prepositions heading the complement.
    • Comparative clauses Ch. 13 §2 Comparative clauses
      The complement of than or as is often a clause, and a reduced one: a comparative clause leaves out material understood from the main clause, always including the expression of the degree being compared.
    • Analytic comparatives with more and less Ch. 13 §4.1 The major governors in comparisons of inequality
      Comparison of inequality is marked either inside the word (taller, tallest) or analytically, with more, most, less and least before an adjective or adverb. Which is used depends mainly on the length and shape of the base.
    • Superlatives and set comparison Ch. 13 §6 Scalar set comparison
      A superlative compares one member of a set with all the others (the oldest house in the street). With a set of only two, the comparative is the usual choice in careful usage (the younger of the two).
    • Non-scalar comparison Ch. 13 §5 Non-scalar comparison
      Some comparisons concern identity or likeness, not degree: the same as, similar to, different from, like. They have their own governors and complements, and no scale is involved.
  14. Chapter 14. Non-finite and verbless clauses pp. 1171–1272
    • Non-finite clauses Ch. 14 §1.1 Matters of form and function
      Non-finite clauses are headed by a plain form (infinitival), gerund-participle or past participle, and usually lack a subject of their own.
    • To-infinitivals Ch. 14 §1.4.2 The infinitival subordinator to
      In to-infinitivals, to is a subordinator marking the VP, not a preposition. A subject, when present, is introduced by the subordinator for.
    • The catenative construction Ch. 14 §1.2 The catenative construction
      Catenative verbs take non-finite clause complements and can form chains (wants to try to leave). In the complex construction an NP intervenes (made Kim laugh).
    • Gerund-participial clauses Ch. 14 §1.5 The structure of gerund-participials
      A gerund-participial clause is headed by an -ing verb and can be subject, complement or adjunct. It is a clause, not an NP, even when it is a subject.
  15. Chapter 15. Coordination and supplementation pp. 1273–1362
    • Coordination Ch. 15 §1.1 Coordinations, coordinates, and coordinators
      A coordination joins coordinates of equal status. The coordinator (and, or, but) is a marker on the coordinate it introduces. Coordinates share a function, not necessarily a category.
    • Supplementation Ch. 15 Coordination and supplementation
      Supplements are loosely attached expressions anchored to a host: appositive NPs, supplementary relatives and interpolations. They are not dependents and are set off by punctuation.
  16. Chapter 16. Information packaging pp. 1363–1448
    • Extraposition Ch. 16 §7.1 The central case: extraposition from subject of content and infinitival clauses
      A subordinate clause subject can be placed at the end, with dummy it in subject position: It is obvious that Kim lied.
  17. Chapter 17. Deixis and anaphora pp. 1449–1564
    • Deixis Ch. 17 §1.1 The concept of deixis
      Deictic expressions are interpreted from the situation of utterance: who is speaking, to whom, where and when. I, you, here, this and that change their reference with every speaker and setting.
    • Anaphora and antecedents Ch. 17 §1.2 Anaphora: anaphors and antecedents
      An anaphor is an expression whose interpretation comes from another expression, its antecedent. Pronouns are the central anaphors, but the antecedent need not be an NP, and an anaphor need not be a pronoun.
    • Order of anaphor and antecedent Ch. 17 §2.4 Order of anaphor and antecedent
      The antecedent usually comes first, but an anaphor can come before it (anticipatory anaphora), most freely when the anaphor is inside a subordinate construction that comes first.
    • Reflexive pronouns Ch. 17 §3 Reflexive pronouns
      Reflexive pronouns (myself, herself, themselves) have two uses: as complements, where they normally need a close antecedent such as the subject of their clause; and as emphatic additions to a clause.
    • Demonstratives Ch. 17 §5 Demonstratives
      This, that, these and those are demonstrative determinatives. Their central use is deictic, contrasting near and far from the speaker; they also point back into the text or conversation (anaphoric use).
    • Pro-forms Ch. 17 §1.4 Pro-forms
      Pro-forms stand in for expressions of many kinds, not only NPs: pro-nominal one replaces a nominal (a blue one), and so can replace a whole clause after verbs like think.
  18. Chapter 18. Inflectional morphology and related matters pp. 1565–1620
    • Lexical bases and inflection Ch. 18 §1.1 Lexical base, morphological operations, and alternation
      An inflected word-form is built from a lexical base by a morphological operation: usually adding a suffix (lamp, lamps), sometimes changing the base itself (goose, geese). The lexeme stays the same; only its form changes.
    • Spelling alternations Ch. 18 §2.2 Spelling alternations
      Adding a suffix can change how the base is written: a final consonant may double (stop, stopping), a silent e may drop (hope, hoping), and y may become i (carry, carries). The base is the same; only its written shape alternates.
    • Inflectional grade Ch. 18 §3 Grade
      Adjectives and some adverbs inflect for grade: plain (big), comparative (bigger) and superlative (biggest). Short bases take the suffixes; longer ones mostly use more and most instead.
    • Plural formation Ch. 18 §4.1 Plural formation
      Most nouns form the plural with ·s, but some modify the base (knife, knives), some have a plural identical to the singular (sheep), and in some compounds the plural is marked inside (passers-by).
    • Regular and irregular verb forms Ch. 18 §5 Verbs
      Regular verbs make the preterite and the past participle with ·ed. Irregular verbs use other operations: a change of vowel (eat, ate), a ·en past participle (eaten), or a mixture of the two.
    • Negative auxiliary forms Ch. 18 §5.5 Negative forms of auxiliaries
      Auxiliary verbs have negative forms such as isn't, didn't and won't. These are inflected forms of the verb, not a verb plus a shortened not, which is why they behave as single words.
  19. Chapter 19. Lexical word-formation pp. 1621–1722
    • Morphological structure Ch. 19 §1.2 Morphological structure
      Complex words have internal structure: bases, to which affixes attach (prefixes before, suffixes after), built up in layers. Unhappiness is un- plus happy first, then plus -ness, not un- plus happiness.
    • Derivational affixation Ch. 19 §5 The core of English lexical word-formation: mainly derivation
      Derivation makes new lexemes by adding affixes: kind gives kindness (adjective to noun), modern gives modernise (adjective to verb). Unlike inflection, it can change the category and the meaning in less predictable ways.
    • Compounds Ch. 19 §4 Compounds
      A compound combines two bases into one word: blackbird, bookshelf, well-known. It can mean something its parts do not predict, and it behaves as a single word, unlike a phrase of the same words.
    • Conversion Ch. 19 §3 Conversion
      Conversion makes a word of a new category with no change of form: the noun bottle gives the verb bottle, the verb walk gives the noun walk. The new lexeme takes the inflections of its new category.
    • Clipping, blending and back-formation Ch. 19 §2 Minor word-formation processes
      Some words are made by shortening or merging rather than by adding affixes: clipping (laboratory to lab), blending (breakfast and lunch to brunch) and back-formation (editor to edit).
  20. Chapter 20. Punctuation pp. 1723–1764
    • Primary terminals Ch. 20 §2 Primary terminals
      A sentence written on its own ends with a primary terminal: a full stop, a question mark or an exclamation mark. The choice signals the kind of speech act, and it need not match the clause type.
    • Commas, semicolons and colons Ch. 20 §3 The secondary boundary marks: comma, semicolon, and colon
      The secondary boundary marks, comma, semicolon and colon, mark boundaries inside a sentence. Each one attaches to a boundary between constituents: the structure decides where a mark can go, and the mark helps the reader find the structure.
    • Delimiting commas, dashes and parentheses Ch. 20 §3.2.5 Delimiting commas
      Some marks set off a constituent on both sides: two commas, two dashes or a pair of parentheses around a supplement. When the supplement ends the sentence, the full stop takes the place of the closing comma or dash.
    • Quotation marks Ch. 20 §6 Quotation marks and related indicators
      Quotation marks set off words presented as someone else's, or as words in their own right. They delimit a constituent like a pair of brackets, but what they signal is the status of the words, not their grammar.
    • Hyphens and word-level punctuation Ch. 20 §8 Word-level punctuation
      Word-level punctuation works inside words: a hyphen joins the parts of some compounds, and an apostrophe marks a genitive or a negative form. Unlike commas, these marks belong to words, not to boundaries between constituents.
