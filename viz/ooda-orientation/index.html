Orient

Destroy the wrong model, act from the better one. What model of the situation should I currently act from, and what should I do next to test or exploit that model?

The interactive planner requires JavaScript. The worksheet and worked examples below work without it.

Orient a situation

  1. What did you actually observe?
  2. What are you currently inferring from it?
  3. What model explains the situation?
  4. What does that model fail to explain?
  5. Which assumption, boundary, goal or object-function can be challenged?
  6. What alternative orientation follows?
  7. What would you expect to observe if it were useful?
  8. What action follows from it?
  9. What result would cause you to reconsider?

You are ready to act when you have a provisional orientation, a next action, an expected observation and a condition for reconsidering.

Three worked examples

A stalled project

Everyday orientation: a full reality → orientation → action → observation chain. A mundane illustrative case written for this page.

Situation
My project keeps slipping even though I spend more time planning it
Observed
  • The last three plans each missed their dates
  • Work expanded beyond each plan's estimate
  • Planning time doubled over two months and throughput did not rise
Inferred
  • I need a better plan
  • The problem is insufficient planning quality
  • About ten hours a week are available for the project
Original model
a planning problem
Unknown
  • Which of the two possible directions for the next version is viable?
Contradiction
  • Previous increases in planning did not improve delivery
Destroyed
  • Negate an assumption: The problem is insufficient planning quality → Uncertainty is too high for a detailed long-range plan to be useful
New orientation
We are actually in a high-uncertainty project where long-range plans hide the open questions. What matters now is the next unresolved uncertainty.
Action
Produce one small artifact or test that exposes the next uncertainty (probe)
Expected signal
The result reveals which of the two possible directions is viable
Reconsider if
The artifact reveals that the uncertainty was already low and execution capacity, not model uncertainty, is the actual constraint
Observed result
A two-day prototype showed one direction fails on real data and the other works; it took two days, not the week I would have planned

Southwest-style boundary reorientation

Boundary destruction: the competitive boundary itself changes. An application from the author's OODA notes, modelled on Southwest Airlines; not a literal Boyd case study, and the details are illustrative.

Situation
A new short-haul airline entering a crowded market
Observed
  • Most trips on our routes are a few hundred kilometres long
  • Most people travelling between these cities drive or take the bus
  • Established airlines on these routes compete on service and schedule
Inferred
  • Airlines flying the same routes
  • Our customers are people who already fly
  • Passengers choose between airlines on service and schedule
  • We are too small to match the incumbents' networks
  • A few aircraft and a simple point-to-point network
Original model
a new airline competing against established airlines
Contradiction
  • Most potential travellers on these routes are not flying at all
Destroyed
  • Move the competitive boundary: Airlines flying the same routes → Short-distance transport for the same journey: cars, buses and airlines
  • Negate an assumption: Our customers are people who already fly → Our customers include people who currently drive or take the bus
New orientation
We are actually in a short-distance transport market where cars and buses are the main competition. What matters now is a fare and frequency that beat driving or the bus door to door.
Rejected candidate
a contest with the established airlines on these routes (it keeps the original boundary, assumptions and move: a rewording, not a new orientation)
Action
Offer a fare and timetable on one route priced against the cost of driving it (maneuver)
Expected signal
A large share of new passengers on that route previously drove or took the bus
Reconsider if
New passengers come almost entirely from other airlines

Boyd's snowmobile

Destroy fixed object/function relationships and recombine the properties. Boyd's own illustration in Destruction and Creation (1976), paraphrased; the component list follows the author's notes.

Situation
Getting across deep snow with what is at hand
Observed
  • None of these objects alone moves a person over deep snow
Inferred
  • Skis
  • Outboard motor
  • Bicycle handlebars
  • Tank tread
  • Skis are for a skier going down a slope
  • An outboard motor drives a boat
  • Handlebars steer a bicycle
  • Tank treads move a tank
  • Each object belongs to its own activity: slope, boat, bicycle, tank
Original model
four unrelated objects, each defined by its usual job
Destroyed
  • Detach an object from its intended function: Skis are for a skier going down a slope → Skis: long low-friction runners that spread weight over snow
  • Detach an object from its intended function: An outboard motor drives a boat → Outboard motor: a compact, portable source of power
  • Detach an object from its intended function: Handlebars steer a bicycle → Handlebars: a hand-held way to steer
  • Detach an object from its intended function: Tank treads move a tank → Tank tread: grip and traction on soft ground
  • Combine properties from several objects: Skis: long low-friction runners that spread weight over snow; Outboard motor: a compact, portable source of power; Handlebars: a hand-held way to steer; Tank tread: grip and traction on soft ground → Snowmobile: runners to glide, a tread for traction, a motor for power and handlebars to steer
New orientation
We are actually in a design problem where parts can be recombined by their properties. What matters now is properties, not names.
Action
Build a rough version and try it on a short stretch of snow (probe)
Expected signal
It moves under its own power and can be steered
Reconsider if
It cannot move or steer on snow

Short cards

Applications and interpretations from Yu Jie Teo's OODA notes, not quotations from Boyd.

  • Nakatomi Space: Repurpose overlooked features of the environment. When an opponent controls every obvious exit and passage, look for what they are not watching, such as ventilation ducts. The environment can be used in ways it was not built for.
  • Monster Chess: Tempo versus material advantage. In monster chess one side moves twice per turn and can hold off a large material advantage for a while. Timing can offset resources, but only temporarily.
  • Reality Signal: Sufficient feedback, not complete awareness. Running the loop needs a reality signal, not full situational awareness. Ask which single signal is enough to tell you whether the model holds.
  • Map and Territory: Model mismatch. The map is not the territory: a model is always incomplete. Look for where the model and the system you are acting on disagree.
  • Boundary Definition: Choose useful system boundaries. The right boundary carves reality at its joints, cutting away irrelevant stimuli and focusing attention where it matters most.
  • Object Function: Break intended-use fixation. Objects and agents are not limited to their intended functions. Recombining them can open strategies outside the usual limits.
  • Overprepared Adversary: Avoid the domain where preparation is strongest. Do not attack where the other side is already heavily prepared; look where their orientation, preparation or awareness is weaker.
  • Deep Memory: Avoid repeating old orientation failures. Keeping a memory of past patterns stops you repeating an orientation that has already failed. This planner checks new candidates against weakened assumptions from earlier loops.
  • Perception Reorientation: Behaviour follows the perceived situation. People act on what they perceive, not on objective reality. Changing what someone can observe can change what they do, without changing material conditions.
  • Tempo vs Orientation: Faster execution does not repair a bad model. High tempo with an inferior orientation can lose to a slower tempo with a better one. Check the model before speeding up.

Why this works this way

What model of the situation should I currently act from, and what should I do next to test or exploit that model?

Action produces new reality; orientation is revised when it fails.

An explanation, not the interface: orientation is the work, the other steps feed and test it.

  1. Write down what you have actually observed, what you infer from it, and what you do not know.
  2. State the model you are currently acting from, with its boundary, assumptions and a prediction.
  3. Test it. If nothing argues against it, keep acting from it and observe.
  4. If it is stale, contradicted or unproductive, reorient: make one to three concrete destructive moves, each with a replacement.
  5. Build two to four candidate orientations from the fragments, contrast them, and adopt one provisionally.
  6. Choose one next action that follows from it, with an expected signal and a reason to reconsider. Starting the action freezes the prediction.
  7. Record what happened next to what you expected, then return to orientation.
Orientation is the centre, not step two (Boyd)
Boyd's later briefings draw orientation as the part of the loop that shapes what gets observed, which decisions look available and how results are read. This page therefore makes the model you act from the main workspace, and treats observe, decide and act as what feeds and tests it.
Destroy, then create (Boyd)
In Destruction and Creation Boyd argues that a concept turned inward on itself drifts out of step with reality, so it has to be broken into parts and the parts recombined into a new concept that fits what is observed. Here that is concrete: each destructive move names what it challenges and requires a replacement, and every candidate cites the fragments it was built from.
Faster action does not repair a faulty orientation (Yu Jie Teo)
Tempo matters, but a fast loop run from the wrong model just produces wrong actions sooner. The page lets you set tempo, compresses prompts when it is high, and warns when tempo rises while failures repeat under an unchanged orientation.
Observations and interpretations are kept apart (This page)
Every item is typed as observed, inferred or unknown. An inference cannot be relabelled as an observation; promoting it records a separate observation. Doubtful observations are qualified, never deleted.
Orientations stay provisional (This page)
Candidates are never declared winners. You adopt one provisionally, rejected candidates stay in the lineage, and confidence is shown next to its evidence rather than standing in for it. There is no score.
Reality, not this page, discriminates models (This page)
The page cannot tell you which orientation is right. It asks for a prediction before you act, freezes it when the action starts, and puts it next to what actually happened.

Teaching notes

When should you redraw the map?

If ordinary corrections keep failing, the problem may no longer be your position on the map. The map itself may be wrong. Routine feedback adjusts the action; reorientation interrupts the routine and changes the model.

Interpretation drawing on Rao's reading of orientation as the part of the loop that generates interrupts and exceptional course corrections, while observe, decide and act run in sequence.

Tempo is not just speed

Tempo is about timing relative to how the situation unfolds, not maximising the number of loops. Sometimes the right tempo is slower.

Interpretation drawing on Rao's book Tempo and his argument that operating faster is not universally better.

Analysis or tinkering?

When goals and plans keep failing, cheap exploratory moves can find what analysis cannot, by breaking assumptions about how resources can be used. A probe is a small, deliberate piece of tinkering.

Interpretation drawing on Rao's essay contrasting goal-driven problem solving with serendipitous tinkering.

When the roadmap keeps slipping

A plan that keeps missing may be a symptom of uncertainty, not of a poor plan. More detail can hide the open question instead of answering it.

This page's interpretation, in the spirit of Rao's critique of goal-driven planning; Rao does not make this exact claim about project plans.

Destruction is a means

Breaking a model apart is useful only if the replacement changes what you can see, expect or do. Destruction without a replacement is not progress.

This page's interpretation of Boyd's Destruction and Creation; the Rao essays are listed for his broader reading of Boyd.

The destruction operations
  • Negate an assumption (Assumptions) — What if the opposite of this were true?
  • Remove an assumption (Assumptions) — What does the model look like with this assumption deleted?
  • Ask what evidence actually supports it (Assumptions) — Which observation, not inference, supports this?
  • Ask what would remain true without it (Assumptions) — What would still be true if this assumption were gone?
  • Remove the goal (Goals, deep reset) — What changes if the goal disappears?
  • Invert the goal (Goals, deep reset) — What would you do if you were trying to cause the opposite?
  • Ask whether the goal belongs to a different game (Goals, deep reset) — Is this goal left over from a game you are no longer playing?
  • Ask what changes if continuing to play is not mandatory (Goals, deep reset) — What becomes possible if you are allowed to stop playing?
  • Widen the system boundary (Boundaries) — What lies just outside this boundary that still shapes the outcome?
  • Narrow the system boundary (Boundaries) — Which part of this boundary actually matters now?
  • Move the competitive boundary (Boundaries) — Why is this the boundary? Who else competes for the same need?
  • Add a previously external actor (Boundaries) — Who outside the current picture affects the result?
  • Remove an irrelevant actor (Boundaries) — Which actor could you leave out without losing anything that matters?
  • Delete a category (Categories) — What do you see if this label did not exist?
  • Merge two categories (Categories) — What if these two kinds were treated as one?
  • Split one category (Categories) — Which different things are hiding under this one label?
  • Ask whether the labels carve reality at useful joints (Categories) — Do these labels follow differences that change what you would do?
  • Reverse actor roles (Actors) — What if the one you think is reacting is actually leading?
  • Split an apparent single actor into several (Actors) — Is this one actor, or several with different incentives?
  • Merge actors with aligned incentives (Actors) — Do these actors effectively want the same thing?
  • Reinterpret who has initiative (Actors) — Who is actually setting the pace here?
  • Detach an object from its intended function (Object / function) — What property matters independently of the object's normal function?
  • Ask what properties it has independently of its name (Object / function) — Describe it without its name: what is it physically, or what can it do?
  • Assign it a new role (Object / function) — What job could this do that nobody has asked it to do?
  • Combine properties from several objects (Object / function) — What new thing do these properties make together?
  • Treat a constraint as a resource (Constraints) — How could this limit work in your favour?
  • Test whether a constraint is actually fixed (Constraints) — What would show this constraint is negotiable?
  • Move the constraint elsewhere (Constraints) — Could this limit bind somewhere it costs less?
  • Change time scale (Constraints) — Is this still a constraint over a week, or over five years?
  • Reverse a causal arrow (Causal stories) — What if the effect is causing the cause?
  • Remove a causal link (Causal stories) — What if these two things are not connected at all?
  • Introduce a common cause (Causal stories) — What third thing could produce both?
  • Ask whether the relation is merely correlated (Causal stories) — Have you seen one change the other, or only seen them together?
  • Remove an information source (Information) — What would you conclude without this signal? (The observation stays in the ledger.)
  • Ask what happens if the signal is wrong (Information) — If this signal were misleading, what would you do differently?
  • Ask what important signal is systematically absent (Information) — What would you expect to see that you never see?
  • Slow down deliberately (Tempo) — What would you notice if you took one more observation before acting?
  • Compress deliberation deliberately (Tempo) — What is the smallest action that would teach you something today?
  • Ask whether speed is compensating for poor orientation (Tempo) — Are you acting faster to avoid admitting the model is wrong?

Examples and sources

Boyd

Ideas attributed to John Boyd: OODA; orientation; destruction and creation; feedback and reorientation; synthesis; tempo. Paraphrased, not quoted.

  • John R. Boyd, Destruction and Creation (1976). Unpublished paper: destructive deduction and creative induction; the snowmobile illustration.
  • John R. Boyd, Patterns of Conflict (briefing, 1986). Tempo and operating inside an opponent's observation-orientation-decision-action time cycle.
  • John R. Boyd, Organic Design for Command and Control (briefing, 1987). Orientation as the focal point that shapes observation, decision and action.
  • John R. Boyd, The Essence of Winning and Losing (briefing, 1995). The loop sketch with feed-forward, feedback and implicit guidance and control from orientation.

The purpose of an OODA loop is not to move through four boxes quickly. It is to maintain an orientation that survives contact with reality, act coherently from that orientation, and use the consequences of action to construct a better one when it fails.
