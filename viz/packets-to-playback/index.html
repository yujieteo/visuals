From Packets to Playback

Probability, information and renormalisation in a live stream

What information about a noisy network actually matters for choosing the next video bitrate?

A live football stream must choose the bitrate of each next segment: 4K, 1080p, 720p or 480p. The risk is a stall: the segment's download time T exceeding the seconds of video already buffered, B. The average download time is not enough; the decision depends on the tail probability P(T > B).

The streaming calculator and the interactive laboratory need JavaScript. Everything below works without it.

One identity

Any probability density can be written as a Boltzmann weight, which is how Euclidean field theory writes its probabilities:

p(x) = Z−1 e−S(x),   so   S(x) = −log p(x) + constant.

The action S is surprisal, the partition function Z is the normalising constant, and W = log Z[J] with a source J generates the cumulants. This is ordinary probability in field-theory notation: the weight is e−S, never the oscillating eiS/ℏ of real-time quantum mechanics.

Coarse-graining

Averaging pairs of 125 ms throughput windows, then pairs of those, and so on, moves from microscopic packets (the UV) to the scale of video segments and playback (the IR). Each step is a renormalisation-group (RG) transformation: the couplings of an effective model change with scale. Features whose influence shrinks are irrelevant; features that grow are relevant. For independent finite-variance windows the flow ends at the Gaussian (the central limit theorem); heavy tails with infinite variance flow to a different, stable law.

RG and the information bottleneck

RG asks which features survive a change of scale. The information bottleneck asks which compressed summary Z of the recent history keeps the most information about the next segment, I(Z; Y), for the least kept, I(Z; X). They often agree on the mean and the persistence of the network. They are not the same operation: the bottleneck also keeps the last half second, a microscopic detail RG would average away, because it predicts this particular segment.

    1The streaming decision

    The next segment carries of video data at the chosen bitrate. With buffered, it stalls if its download time T exceeds the buffer: the risk is P(T > B), a tail probability.

    Simplification: a segment downloads at the average throughput over one segment-length stretch of windows; lost packets are resent, so it carries 1 / (1 − loss) times its size.

    Why is the mean not enough?

    Two networks with the same mean download time can have very different chances of exceeding B. The decision depends on the shape of the distribution beyond B: on its tail, and on how strongly bad windows cluster in time. Knowing only E[T] throws that away.

    2A distribution, and its action

    Start with the simplest candidate: each 125 ms window's throughput b is Gaussian around the recent average μ with spread σ. Taking minus the log of the density gives a quadratic, the :

    p(b) ∝ exp(−(b − μ)² / 2σ²)  ⇔  S(b) = (b − μ)² / 2σ² + constant

    density p(b)action S(b) = −log p(b) + C

    Low action means likely; each extra unit of action makes a value e times less likely. The constant is log , the normalising sum, and it never changes which value is more likely.

    Derive S = −log p + C

    Write any positive density as p(x) = e−S(x)/Z with Z = ∫ e−S(x) dx. Taking logs, S(x) = −log p(x) − log Z. In information theory −log p(x) is the surprisal of x (in nats; divide by ln 2 for bits), so the action is surprisal up to a constant. Nothing quantum has happened: this is a change of notation.

    3Heavy tails

    Four candidate models share the same typical middle half of windows and differ in the tails. None is claimed to describe Internet traffic in general; they are hypotheses to compare.

    A modelling choice: the families are matched on their interquartile range, not their variance, so the tails add variance (the sd / mean column). Matched on variance instead, a heavy tail must take probability out of moderate dips to pay for its rare deep ones, which hides the effect this step is about.

    Derive the couplings

    For a stationary Gaussian AR(1) chain, bt+1 − μ = ρ(bt − μ) + noise, the action on a ring of windows is Σ (yt+1 − ρyt)² / 2σ²(1 − ρ²) with y = b − μ. Expanding −2ρytyt+1 = ρ[(yt+1 − yt)² − yt² − yt+1²] gives exactly

    g2 = (1 − ρ) / 2σ²(1 + ρ),   g∇ = ρ / 2σ²(1 − ρ²).

    So g2 is the inverse fluctuation scale and g∇ penalises jumps between neighbouring windows: the larger it is, the more persistent the network. The tail term is not Gaussian; its coupling is reported through the excess kurtosis it produces, the fourth . The non-Gaussian models here use a Gaussian copula, so their exact action is −Σ log f(bt) plus a quadratic term in the latent variables; the three-term action above is a truncated summary of it.

    5Sources, Z[J] and W[J]

    Add a J: reweight every outcome by eJX. For X = a segment's average throughput, a negative J leans the distribution toward slow segments, the stall side. This is :

    pJ(x) = eJx p(x) / Z[J],   Z[J] = E[eJX],   W[J] = log Z[J]

    original p(x)tilted pJ(x)tangent: slope W′(J)

    Derive W′ = ⟨X⟩J and W″ = VarJ(X)

    W′(J) = Z′/Z = E[XeJX]/E[eJX] = ⟨X⟩J. Differentiating again, W″ = ⟨X²⟩J − ⟨X⟩J² = VarJ(X). At J = 0 the derivatives of W are the cumulants κn: mean, variance, third cumulant, and so on. In field theory W generates the ; here it is the cumulant generating function. The page computes all three numbers directly from the 4,096 synthetic segments, so the identities hold exactly for that empirical distribution.

    Optional: Feynman diagrams as bookkeeping

    For a Gaussian, every moment reduces to pairings (Wick's theorem): E[x⁴] = 3σ⁴ because four points pair up in three ways. Add a weak quartic coupling, S = x²/2 + λx⁴/24, and expand e−λx⁴/24 in λ. Each term is a sum of pairings, and a diagram is a picture of one pairing: lines are pairings (propagators), dots are vertices (factors of λ).

    The diagrams are bookkeeping devices for terms in an expansion, not trajectories of particles. The exact values come from numerical integration; the first-order values from the two diagrams.

    6Coarse-graining

    Replace each pair of windows by its average. That is exact: no information about the averages is lost, only detail inside each pair is . Repeat it and watch what happens to the shape of the distribution. With the central-limit normalisation (X₁ + X₂)/√2 of independent draws, finite-variance shapes flow to the Gaussian; an infinite-variance tail does not.

    10Rate–distortion

    Video coding is lossy. For a Gaussian source the rate needed for mean-squared distortion D is R(D) = ½ log₂(σ² / D) bits per sample. The page treats a picture schematically as such a source with one bit per sample costing R₀ = 4 Mbps, so quality Q = 1 − D/σ² = 1 − 2−2R/R₀.
