The risk-neutral density is the curvature of the call-price curve.

Differentiate a call price twice with respect to its strike K. Times erT, that second derivative is the market-implied probability density of the stock ending at that strike. The kink in a call’s payoff is the reason: its second distributional derivative is a Dirac delta.

Synthetic example. Call prices here are Black–Scholes prices (S0 = 100, r = 5%, σ = 20%, T = 1 year), not market data. Slide the strike and shrink the butterfly half-width Δ to watch curvature become density.

The independent check: three routes, one number

At K = 100, the butterfly portfolio (call@K−Δ − 2 call@K + call@K+Δ) has a tent payoff that concentrates on a Dirac delta, so its normalized price must approach the density. The finite difference of the synthetic call curve below and the closed-form Black–Scholes density both confirm it:

Closed form: p(K) = φ(d2) / (Kσ√T) with d2 = 0.15 at K = 100, giving p(100) = 0.019724. The call price C(100) = 10.4506, its second derivative C″(100) = 0.018762, and erT C″(100) = 0.019724 — the butterflies converge onto the same value as Δ → 0.

Honesty note. Every price on this page is a synthetic Black–Scholes number (S0 = 100, r = 5%, σ = 20%, T = 1y). The Breeden–Litzenberger identity itself is model-free: it only needs call prices that are convex in strike, so their second derivative exists almost everywhere.

Sources.

  1. Prices of State-Contingent Claims Implicit in Option Prices, Douglas T. Breeden and Robert H. Litzenberger, 1978. Original derivation: the state-price density equals the second derivative of the call price with respect to strike.
  2. Options and the Gamma Knife, Ian Martin, 2018. Inspected at equation (1) and footnote 2: price of a claim to g(S_T) equals the integral of g(K) times the second derivative of the call price in K; call prices are convex so the second derivative exists almost everywhere.
  3. Heaviside step function. Inspected: d/dx max{x,0} = H(x) and the distributional derivative dH/dx = delta(x), hence the second distributional derivative of max{x,0} is the Dirac delta.
