Words hide numbers

In 1951 an American intelligence estimate called a Soviet attack on Yugoslavia “a serious possibility”. Sherman Kent, who helped write it, meant odds of about 65 to 35 in favour; a policy reader took it to mean much lower odds, and the board that agreed the wording had meant anything from 20 to 80 to 80 to 20. In 1964 Kent proposed agreeing on the vocabulary of probability first:

Narrated report of this estimate, its updates and forecasts for beamdswitch:

    Sherman Kent

    Intelligence estimates frequently have to express judgments under uncertainty. Kent argued that phrases such as “probable” or “almost certain” are dangerous when writers and readers silently attach different probabilities to them.

    His proposed vocabulary attached several common expressions to explicit regions on a probability scale. This visual turns that idea into an interactive reasoning tool.

    Kent 1964, as printed

    100% certainty; 93% give or take almost 6%, almost certain; 75% give or take about 12%, probable; 50% give or take about 10%, chances about even; 30% give or take about 10%, probably not; 7% give or take about 5%, almost certainly not; 0% impossibility. The bands here are those give-or-take ranges (87–99, 63–87, 40–60, 20–40, 2–12). Kent’s chart names the ends “certainty” and “impossibility”; this tool says “certain” and “impossible”. The bands leave gaps (0–2, 12–20, 60–63, 99–100), shown as gaps rather than filled in.

    Synonyms recognised in free English are only those in the essay’s own synonym table (for example “likely” and “we believe” under probable; “unlikely” and “doubtful” under probably not). Kent notes “unlikely” meant odds of about three to one against.

    Only Kent 1964 is implemented. Later vocabularies (the modern US Intelligence Community, NATO, the UK PHIA yardstick) use different words and bands and are not merged into it.
