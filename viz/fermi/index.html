Fermi estimator

Break a hard question into rough quantities. Get the scale right before worrying about precision.

Estimate by breaking the question down

A Fermi estimate answers a question you cannot look up by splitting it into a few smaller quantities you can guess, then multiplying, dividing or adding them. The aim is the right order of magnitude (tens, hundreds or thousands), not decimal places.

Worked example: a restaurant queue

How long will this restaurant queue take?

12 groups
× 10 minutes per cycle
÷ 4 groups per cycle
≈ 30 minutes

Unsure of the numbers? Give each one a range: 8 to 16 groups ahead, 8 to 14 minutes per seating cycle, 3 to 5 groups seated each cycle. The lowest combination, 8 × 8 ÷ 5, gives about 13 minutes; the highest, 16 × 14 ÷ 3, about 75 minutes. So the answer is about 30 minutes, with a plausible range of 13 to 75 minutes.

That range comes from the low and high values chosen. It is not a measured confidence interval: the answer is only as good as the assumptions.

    How Fermi estimation works

    Decompose before calculating. The hard part is choosing the smaller quantities that determine the answer: things × amount per thing, rate × time, total ÷ capacity.

    Ranges before fake precision. Give each factor a low, best and high value you would not be surprised by. The lowest result takes each multiplied factor at its low and each divisor at its high; the highest does the opposite; + terms add. No random sampling is used.

    Orders of magnitude. Results show about two significant figures, one when the range spans ten times or more, on a log scale that shows tens, hundreds or thousands at a glance.

    Sensitivity. Each factor moves across its own range with the others at best, scored by the logarithm of the ratio of the two results, so doubling counts the same at any scale. It shows where a better guess helps most, not what causes the result.

    The answer is only as good as these assumptions, and the range is not a measured confidence interval. Everything runs on your device.

    Common Fermi patterns

    Rate × time

    total ≈ rate × time

    Customers a day, distance travelled, money spent, data generated.

    Population × fraction

    target group ≈ population × relevant fraction

    Total ÷ capacity

    time, batches or resources ≈ total ÷ capacity

    Density × area

    count ≈ density × area

    Parts × amount per part

    total ≈ number of parts × amount per part
