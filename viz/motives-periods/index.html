Motives and periods

A period is a number you get by integrating a rational form over a rational domain. Each one comes from a motive. Compute three kinds of period here, and see the motive behind each number.

Marcolli’s question: are residues of Feynman integrals periods of mixed Tate motives?

The third example answers it for the smallest graphs. The first two build the vocabulary: the Tate period 2πi, then multiple zeta values.

Three periods, without JavaScript

2πi. The loop around 0 and the form dz/z on Gm = P¹ − {0, ∞} pair to ∮ dz/z = 2πi, the period of the Tate motive. On the projective line h(P¹) = 𝟏 ⊕ 𝟏(−1), and counting points over Fq reads this as #P¹(Fq) = 1 + q.

ζ(3). ζ(3) = Σ 1/n³ = ∫1>t₁>t₂>t₃>0 dt₁dt₂dt₃ / (t₁t₂(1 − t₃)) = 1.202056903159594. Every multiple zeta value is a period of a mixed Tate motive over ℤ.

Feynman periods. The wheel with three spokes, K₄, has residue ∫ Ω/Ψ² = 6ζ(3) = 7.212341418958, and its graph polynomial has q⁵ + q³ − q² zeros over Fq.

The motive. h(P¹) = 𝟏 ⊕ 𝟏(−1). Removing 0 and ∞ leaves Gm: dz/z spans H¹ = 𝐐(−1), the loop spans H₁ = 𝐐(1), and their pairing 2πi is the period of the Tate motive.

Counting points sends 𝟏 ↦ 1 and 𝐋 = 𝟏(−1) ↦ q:

Derivation

Parametrise the loop as z(θ) = c + eiθ. The integral of dz/z changes log z continuously along the loop, so it is i times the total change of arg z: 2π for each turn around 0, and 0 when 0 lies outside. The Riemann sum replaces dz by zj+1 − zj and 1/z by 1/zj; its error falls like 1/steps.

Why 2πi is the period of the Tate motive: the de Rham class of dz/z and the Betti class of the loop are rational bases of the two realisations of the same one-dimensional motive, and the comparison between them is multiplication by 2πi. Multiplying a period by a power of 2πi is a Tate twist.

      The motive. Every multiple zeta value is a period of a mixed Tate motive over ℤ, and every such period is a ℚ[(2πi)⁻¹]-combination of them. The weight bounds how many are independent:

      Derivation

      Expand 1/(1 − t) = Σ tⁿ and integrate the word from the innermost variable outward: each x₁ starts a new summation index and each x₀ divides by it once more, which rebuilds the nested sum Σn₁>⋯>nk 1/(n₁s₁⋯nksk). Substituting t ↦ 1 − t in every variable reverses the order of the variables and swaps dt/t with dt/(1 − t), which is duality.

      The plain sum converges slowly (its tail is about N1−s₁). The page’s value instead splits the iterated integral at t = 1/2; both halves are multiple polylogarithms at 1/2, whose terms shrink like 2⁻ⁿ, so 64 terms reach double precision.

          The motive. Counting zeros of ΨG is the point-count realisation of the graph hypersurface. A mixed Tate motive counts as a polynomial in q:

          Answer to the question, for these graphs: yes. Each period here is a rational multiple of a zeta value, and the wheels’ cohomology is Tate. In general: not for every graph. Point counts stop being polynomial (Belkale–Brosnan), and in φ⁴ itself Brown and Schnetz found graphs whose counts follow a modular form from a K3 surface.

          Derivation

          Schwinger parameters turn each propagator 1/k² into ∫ e−αk² dα; doing the loop momenta leaves ∫ Ω/ΨG² on the simplex of the αe, where ΨG sums, over spanning trees, the product of the αe left out. A graph with twice as many edges as loops and no divergent subgraph gives a finite number: its residue.

          For K₄ the page checks 6ζ(3) independently. Completing K₄ to K₅ and placing three vertices at 0, 1 and ∞ in ℝ⁴ leaves two integrated vertices; one is done in closed form (the graphical function 4iD(z)/(z − z̄), with D the Bloch–Wigner dilogarithm), and the page integrates the other over the half-plane with Gauss–Legendre quadrature.
